## A chart is a claim

> A chart is a claim about how the world is organised.

Prompt: What has the designer chosen to make visible? What remains invisible?

---

## What we will be able to do

- distinguish evidence, encoding, observation, insight and narrative;
- identify unsupported or overconfident interpretation;
- explain how visual choices shape meaning;
- verify and repair an AI-assisted chart interpretation.

---

## Predict before looking

Four datasets share the same:

- mean and variance;
- correlation;
- regression line.

Prompt: How similar do you expect their plots to look?

## Anscombe’s quartet

Show the four panels without commentary.

Prompts:

- What does the summary conceal?
- Which panel most changes your interpretation?
- What should have been checked before making a claim?

## From evidence to narrative

```text
DATA → ENCODING → OBSERVATION → CLAIM → NARRATIVE
```

At every transition: selection, comparison, compression, framing and omission.

---

## Observation is not explanation

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| Observation | Deaths appear concentrated near one pump |
| Interpretation | Exposure to that pump may be associated with illness |
| Causal claim | Contaminated pump water caused the outbreak |

Prompt: What extra evidence is required at each step?

## Human and machine interpretation

Humans and models can both:

- notice patterns;
- impose familiar stories;
- ignore missing context;
- confuse association with cause;
- communicate uncertainty poorly.

Difference: responsibility for a submitted claim remains with the student.

---

## Fluent does not mean valid

Place an AI-generated interpretation beside a chart.

Mark each sentence:

- supported directly;
- plausible but unverified;
- unsupported;
- contradicted by the data.

---

## Verification ladder

**Trace:** What data and transformation produced the chart?  
**Check:** Do labels, units, scales and totals agree?  
**Test:** Does the pattern survive another view?  
**Bound:** What uncertainty qualifies the claim?  
**Disclose:** What did the intelligent tool contribute?

---

## Representation is governance

Use the AIATSIS acknowledgement discussion.

- Who is represented?
- Who collected and controls the data?
- Who benefits from the visualisation?
- Who could be harmed or misled?
- What should not be visualised?

---

## The visual grammar

Introduce Bertin’s variables:

position · size · value · texture · colour · orientation · shape

Prompt: Which variables support precise comparison? Which are better for grouping?

## Notebook lab

Students will:

- calculate Anscombe’s shared statistics;
- plot all four datasets;
- constrain an AI-generated interpretation;
- label claims by evidence status;
- make and repair one misleading design choice.

---

## Critique and Repair

Identify:

- claim and audience;
- visual task and encoding;
- evidence and omissions;
- overclaim or possible harm;
- verification needed;
- one concrete repair.
